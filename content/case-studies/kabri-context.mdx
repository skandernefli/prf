*Understanding the business environment, identifying the operational problems, and defining the direction of the solution.*

## 01. Overview

### The business

KABRI is a Canadian company specializing in the design, manufacturing, and commercialization of cabanons (sheds) and related structures.

Its business operations involve managing customers, handling product requirements, configuring customized products, preparing quotations, and coordinating the information required to move from a customer request to an order ready for production.

Unlike businesses selling standardized products, customized manufacturing introduces additional complexity. Product specifications, dimensions, configurations, options, pricing, and customer requirements must remain consistent throughout the commercial and operational processes.

### The project

The objective of this project was to develop a centralized full-stack application capable of supporting the company's business operations through a unified digital environment.

The application was designed around several core business capabilities:

- Customer and business management.
- Custom product configuration.
- Product and configuration management.
- Business operations and associated workflows.
- Centralized information management.
- [Additional business capabilities to document.]

The challenge was not simply to develop another management interface. It was to understand how the company's business processes worked, identify the limitations of the existing environment, and translate those requirements into a coherent software solution.

The software must therefore reflect the actual business domain instead of imposing an artificial technical structure on it.

## 03. Before the project came to me

### The existing environment

Before introducing the new application, the company's operational environment needs to be examined to understand how information was created, stored, accessed, and transferred.

The investigation should identify whether existing applications, spreadsheets, databases, manual procedures, or disconnected workflows were being used.

For each existing system, the following questions must be answered:

- What responsibility does this system handle?
- Which business users depend on it?
- What information does it store?
- Does it communicate with other systems?
- Are users required to manually transfer information?
- Does it duplicate information already maintained elsewhere?
- What happens when information changes?
- Which business processes depend on it?

Such workflows create dependencies on employees, increase the possibility of inconsistent information, and make it difficult to maintain a reliable overview of business operations.

The objective of this analysis is to identify not only what applications exist, but also how the company actually operates through them.

## 05. The proposed solution

### One centralized application

The proposed direction was to develop a centralized application that brings the company's relevant business operations into a unified environment.

Instead of requiring employees to navigate disconnected applications and independently maintained information, the platform would provide a common foundation for managing customers, products, configurations, and associated business processes.

### Solution principles

**Centralized information management**

Business entities should have a consistent representation within the application, reducing unnecessary duplication and providing a common source of information.

**Connected business operations**

Features should interact through defined relationships and workflows rather than operating as isolated modules.

**Flexible product configuration**

The platform should support the company's product customization requirements while maintaining consistency between product definitions, configuration options, and associated information.

**Maintainable architecture**

The application should be structured so that business capabilities can evolve without requiring unrelated parts of the platform to be redesigned.

**Operational usability**

The interface should reflect the actual responsibilities and workflows of business users, making complex operations accessible through a coherent experience.

### Expected transformation

The intended transformation is a move from disconnected operational processes toward a centralized application in which business information and operations can be managed within a shared environment.

## 06. Existing environment and feasibility study

Before implementation, the existing environment must be studied in enough detail to determine what should be retained, integrated, replaced, or redesigned.

This phase establishes the technical and operational boundaries of the project.

### Business requirements investigation

Each business requirement should be translated into an explicit functional responsibility.

For example, customer management requires more than displaying a customer list. It requires understanding how customer information is created, updated, associated with business operations, and retrieved throughout the application.

Similarly, product configuration requires identifying which properties are configurable, which combinations are valid, and how configuration information is represented and persisted.

### Existing technology assessment

The assessment should cover:

- Existing databases and data structures.
- Existing application interfaces.
- Available APIs and external integrations.
- Authentication and access management.
- Existing data formats and business rules.

## 07. Implementation strategy and project plan

### Defining the implementation approach

Once the business requirements and existing environment have been understood, the next step is to translate them into an implementation plan.

The application should be developed around clearly defined business capabilities, with dependencies between modules identified before implementation begins.

A possible decomposition is:

1. Core application foundation.
2. Customer and business management.
3. Product and configuration management.
4. Business workflows and operations.
5. API integration and data communication.
6. Validation, testing, and refinement.
7. Deployment and stabilization.

### Dependency analysis

The implementation order matters.

For example, product configuration depends on a clear understanding of product definitions and their relationships. Similarly, business workflows may depend on customer and product information being available through consistent interfaces.

Developing dependent modules without first establishing their underlying data structures can introduce unnecessary rework.

### Scope management

Each feature should be associated with:

- A defined business requirement.
- Its corresponding application responsibility.
- Its dependencies.
- Its expected inputs and outputs.
- Its validation rules.
- Its acceptance criteria.

This creates traceability between what the business needs and what the engineering team implements.
